:::foreword
A system that thinks back changes the interface between you and it. These notes ask why to design for it, and what exactly changes.
:::

Buttons and forms assumed a system that was fast, deterministic, and silent about its own uncertainty. A model-backed interface can be none of those things. This thesis asks what it means to design when the system on the other side thinks, or performs enough of the appearance of thinking that the distinction stops mattering to the person using it. The shift reaches past the visual language of a product. It rewrites the contract between interface and user: the pacing of interaction, and the kind of trust a screen is asking for. Pretending the old contract still holds is where most of the current generation of AI products goes wrong.

What is collected here is neither a style guide nor a manifesto. These are working notes from a practice that is still figuring itself out, and each chapter began as a specific design problem in a specific prototype. A chatbot felt dishonest about what it knew. A navigation system had to double as model context. An image generator's interface hid the very iteration it was meant to support.

You might expect these problems to resolve into a single unified theory of AI interface design. They do not, because no such theory yet deserves to exist. What the thesis offers instead is a set of vocabularies, principles, and case studies precise enough to be argued with.

After this preface, the chapters are organized in two movements. Foundations lays the conceptual groundwork: how to represent a thinking process on screen, how a styleguide accounts for content it did not author, and what principles hold across cases. It also names the interaction primitives that recur once submission and response stop being instantaneous and certain. UIs then works through specific surfaces: a FAQ, an endless article, a moodboard, a sketch pad, a filtered list, and a dashboard. Each is a small independent study, not a subordinate example of the foundations. Read the foundations first for the argument in order. Start with a UI chapter to see the argument tested against something concrete.

The method throughout is practice-based. Build a working prototype, put it in front of people, and let what breaks show which terms of the contract the interface actually needs to state. That produces a different kind of evidence than a survey of best practices would, and a more honest one. Several positions here are provisional, flagged as such where it matters, and likely to look naive in a few years. That is an acceptable cost of writing about a technology this early in its interface history. What is not provisional is the commitment to design against the system's actual behavior, not its marketing description.

A reader might take a thesis about AI interfaces as a case for more AI. It is not. Nothing here assumes that these interfaces are inherently good for users, or that more of the technology is automatically the right design choice. Several chapters argue for restraint: not streaming when a static answer would be clearer, not personifying a system that has no persistent self, not hiding uncertainty behind confident prose. The thesis is optimistic about the medium and skeptical of most of what has shipped in it so far. Those two positions are not in tension. Holding both at once is, in a sense, the whole argument.

:::notes
Read sequentially or jump to any chapter; each stands on its own. Where a chapter cites a prototype or study, that is the source for the claim being made, not an illustrative aside.
:::
