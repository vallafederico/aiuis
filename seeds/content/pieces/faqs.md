:::foreword
Every question on this site is answered from the chapters themselves, which enter the index as they are published.
:::

The last row on the list is empty, and it is not a chat. Type a question there and one of three things happens: a row already on the list opens, a new row joins the list and cites its chapter, or nothing appears at all.

You might expect a FAQ to be a second text, written beside a site to explain it. Here it is a way of reading the argument, not a second corpus beside it. aiu.is is the research project, not a brochure for one. The pages are the prototypes and the chapters are the argument: what an interface owes a person when the system on the other side thinks, or only performs thinking well enough that the difference stops mattering.

The list is therefore closed over the published text. content.software indexes each chapter when it is published: the canonical markdown, searchable by the words in it and by meaning. A question runs that search, and the row that comes back names the chapter its passage came from. When no passage covers the question, no row appears. Silence is the honest result. A generated sentence the project never wrote would be a different product.

That constraint is the research claim in small form. The index is the project's notebook, and the interface should select from what has actually been written in it. Generation is the exception, and it comes only after the index has been consulted. Most questions about this site already have a chapter: what the project is, why a wait state should not pretend to be thought, why the navigation is also the model's scope. Those answers sit in the index because they sit in the chapters. They are not stored in a parallel FAQ file that can drift from the page.

What you ask stays in this browser. Another machine starts from the same published index, not from your list.

The same rule decides what an article on this site is for. A chapter describes this project: the prototype on the page, and what the rest of the site has already said. Text that does not come from that body is not part of the research, and the index will not pretend that it is.

The obvious objection: why not let the model answer from what it already knows, and point to the nearest chapter? Clark and Chalmers (1998) argue that a notebook belongs to a cognitive system when it is consulted as a matter of course, and when the information there is trusted. The index meets that test only if the FAQ actually reads it before it speaks. A model that answers from general habit, and cites a chapter it did not retrieve, fails the test. The chapter is then decoration. The research requires the opposite order: retrieve, then speak, and stay quiet when retrieval fails.

:::notes
Clark, Andy, and David Chalmers. The Extended Mind. Analysis, 1998.
Search runs on the published pieces collection. A miss does not invent a chapter.
:::
