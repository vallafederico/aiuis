:::foreword
A question on this site is answered from the chapters, which are indexed as they are published.
:::

aiu.is is the research project, not a brochure for one. The pages are the prototypes, and the chapters are the argument: what an interface owes a person when the system on the other side thinks, or only performs thinking well enough that the difference stops mattering. A FAQ on that site is a way of reading the argument, not a second corpus beside it.

The list is therefore closed over the published text. content.software indexes each chapter when it is published: the canonical markdown, searchable by the words in it and by meaning. Asking a question runs that search. The row that comes back names the chapter the passage came from. If the index has no passage that covers the question, the row does not appear. Silence is the honest result. A generated sentence that the project never wrote would be a different product.

That constraint is the research claim in small form. The interface should select from what has actually been written here. Generation is the exception, and only after the index has been consulted. Most questions about this site have a chapter already: what the project is, why a wait state should not pretend to be thought, why the navigation is also the model's scope. Those answers are in the index because they are in the chapters. They are not stored as a parallel FAQ file that can drift from the page.

The empty last row is the ask. It is not a chat. If the question matches a row already on the list, that row opens. If the index returns a passage, a new row joins the list and cites its chapter. What you ask stays in this browser. Another machine starts from the same published index, not from your list.

The same rule is what an article on this site is for. A chapter describes this project: the prototype on the page, and what the rest of the site has already said. Text that does not come from that body is not part of the research, and the index will not pretend that it is.

Clark and Chalmers (1998) argue that a notebook belongs to a cognitive system when it is consulted as a matter of course, and when the information there is trusted. The index meets that test only if the FAQ actually reads it before it speaks. A model that answers from general habit, and cites a chapter it did not retrieve, fails the test. The chapter is then decoration. The research requires the opposite order: retrieve, then speak, and stay quiet when retrieval fails.

:::notes
Clark, Andy, and David Chalmers. The Extended Mind. Analysis, 1998.

:::
